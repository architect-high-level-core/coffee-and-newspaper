Two very different regulatory stories are unfolding on either side of the Atlantic. In Brussels, the EU AI Act is moving from law on paper to practical machinery: rules for test environments, templates and enforcement plans. In Washington, the 3 November midterm election has reshuffled the fight over who gets to regulate AI at all – the federal government or the states.

This article explains what was actually decided in November 2026, what is still only proposed, and where commentators are making predictions.

## The short version

- **EU, fact:** common rules for *AI regulatory sandboxes* enter into force on **1 December 2026** (Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) 2026/2487).
- **EU, fact:** the Commission is collecting feedback on a draft **template for fundamental rights impact assessments**, which certain users of high-risk AI must complete from **2 December 2027**.
- **EU, fact:** Europe's data protection authorities chose **AI-driven candidate screening in recruitment** for a coordinated GDPR enforcement action in 2027.
- **US, fact:** Democrats won control of both chambers of Congress. **Prediction (widely shared by analysts):** a federal law that overrides state AI laws is now unlikely before 2029.
- **US, fact:** the administration continues to challenge state AI laws through an executive order, the Justice Department and agency guidance. Governors have proposed a split: federal rules for the most powerful models, state rules for consumers, workers and children.

## Europe: the AI Act gets its machinery

The **AI Act** (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689) is the EU's comprehensive AI law. It sorts AI uses by risk: some practices are banned, "high-risk" systems (for example in hiring, credit or critical infrastructure) face strict duties, and many other systems only have transparency obligations. The law applies in stages, which is why much of the current news is about implementation details rather than new rules.

### New sandbox rules from 1 December

A **regulatory sandbox** is a supervised test environment: a company can develop and test an AI system with guidance from a national authority before putting it on the market.

According to the [Official Journal entry on EUR-Lex](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg_impl/2026/2487/oj/eng) and a summary by the compliance newsletter [Veritome](https://veritome.eu/brief/2026-w47), Implementing Regulation (EU) 2026/2487 was adopted on 10 November, published on 11 November and enters into force on 1 December 2026. Key points:

- **Who can apply:** *providers* (the companies that build an AI system and put it on the market under their name) or prospective providers. Organisations that only *use* an AI system cannot apply alone, but can join a provider's application.
- **Eligibility:** systems not yet on the market, systems facing a substantial modification, or systems that became high-risk through a change of purpose.
- **Speed:** authorities must decide within three months of a complete application.
- **Cost:** access to listed services is free of charge and prioritised for small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs), including start-ups.
- **Limits:** the exit report after a sandbox is documentation, not a certificate of conformity. Participants remain fully responsible for complying with EU law.

### A template for fundamental-rights checks

On 16 November the [European Commission](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/news/commission-seeks-feedback-draft-template-fundamental-rights-impact-assessment-under-ai-act) opened a consultation on a draft questionnaire for the **fundamental rights impact assessment** under Article 27 of the AI Act. This is a check that certain *deployers* (users) of high-risk AI systems, such as public bodies, must carry out before using the system, asking who could be affected and how harms are prevented.

The Commission says the obligation applies **from 2 December 2027**. The draft was written by the EU AI Office with input from the EU Agency for Fundamental Rights, and the Commission is also looking for deployers who want to test it in practice.

### The bigger timeline

Two dates matter for most organisations:

- The AI Act's **transparency obligations** in Article 50 (for example, telling people when they interact with an AI system or see AI-generated content) apply under the Act's timeline from **2 August 2026**.
- The obligations for **stand-alone high-risk systems** listed in Annex III apply from **2 December 2027**. According to [Veritome](https://veritome.eu/brief/2026-w47), this date reflects a deferral made by Regulation (EU) 2026/1744.

### Privacy regulators target AI in hiring

Separately from the AI Act, the **European Data Protection Board (EDPB)**, the body that coordinates national privacy authorities, announced on 11 November that its 2027 coordinated enforcement action will cover automated decision-making in recruitment, including AI-driven candidate screening ([summary via Veritome](https://veritome.eu/brief/2026-w47)). The legal basis is the GDPR, the EU's data protection law, so it does not wait for the 2027 AI Act deadline. In practice, employers will need to explain whether hiring decisions are made "solely" by software and what logic is involved.

### Governance bodies get organised

The Commission also reported that the [AI Board](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/news/ai-board-holds-its-tenth-meeting), where member states coordinate, held its tenth meeting on 18 November and adopted new rules of procedure with an elected chair for two-year terms. The [AI Act Advisory Forum](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/news/ai-act-advisory-forum-holds-its-second-meeting), which represents industry and civil society, held its second meeting and was invited to advise on possible work on AI companions and AI agents.

**Our assessment (opinion):** none of this is dramatic, and that is the point. The EU is building templates, procedures and test environments now, so that the high-risk rules can actually be applied in 2027.

## United States: the preemption fight after the midterms

The central US question is **preemption**: whether a federal law should override ("preempt") the AI laws that individual states have passed. The tech industry and the administration have pushed for a single national rulebook; many states and consumer groups want to keep their own protections.

### What the election changed (fact)

According to a briefing by the law firm [Faegre Drinker](https://www.faegredrinker.com/en/insights/publications/2026/11/how-the-midterm-results-could-reshape-ai-and-data-center-regulations-and-more-developments), Democrats took control of both chambers of Congress on 3 November. The firm cites an exit poll of 100,000 voters in which 56% said AI and data centers were a "crucial or major factor" in their vote, with two-thirds of those voters backing Democrats.

An analysis in [American Intelligencer](https://americanintelligencer.com/preemption-died-on-election-night/), citing Associated Press race calls, puts the new Senate at 56 Democrats to 44 Republicans from January, short of the two-thirds needed to override a presidential veto. It quotes President Trump on the *Pod Force One* podcast: "I'll become Mr. Veto."

### Why both sides can now block each other (analysis)

The same analysis describes two competing versions of preemption:

- **The administration's version:** a "minimally burdensome national standard" that would override state laws. It needs Congress, which Democrats will control from January.
- **The Democratic version:** a strong federal standard, for example independent audits before powerful models are released, that might justify replacing state rules. It needs a president willing to sign it.

**Prediction:** Faegre Drinker writes that "most analysts expect change to come first through oversight" – hearings and document requests – rather than new laws, and that broad preemption "now looks unlikely". The remaining short-term risk is the "lame duck" session before the new Congress starts on 3 January. A preemption clause could theoretically be attached to the annual defense bill or a government funding bill in December, but American Intelligencer found nothing in the public record that puts one there yet.

### The executive route (fact)

The administration did not wait for Congress. **Executive Order 14365**, signed on 11 December 2025, created a Justice Department task force to challenge state AI laws, tied leftover broadband funding to a list of "onerous" state laws, and asked the FCC and FTC to look at federal rules that would override state ones ([American Intelligencer](https://americanintelligencer.com/preemption-died-on-election-night/)). The article reports that the Justice Department moved to join xAI's lawsuit against Colorado's AI law in April, that Colorado repealed and re-enacted the law in May, and that the FTC proposed a policy statement on preemption in July.

An executive order is not a law, so these steps are decided case by case – in court, through grants or through agency rules – rather than settling the question nationwide.

### The states keep writing the rules

Without a federal law, the states set the pace. California's **SB 53** (the Transparency in Frontier Artificial Intelligence Act, signed in September 2025) and New York's **RAISE Act** (signed in December 2025) require the largest AI developers to publish safety information and report serious incidents.

On 22 November, the bipartisan **National Governors Association** proposed a division of labour, as reported by [Bloomberg Law](https://news.bloomberglaw.com/artificial-intelligence/governors-call-for-federal-ai-rules-without-sidelining-states): Washington should regulate the most powerful AI systems, including independent safety reviews and mandatory reporting of serious security and safety incidents, while states remain responsible for consumer protection, anti-discrimination rules and the impact of AI on jobs.

### An AI company drafts its own bill

One unusual development: according to [Faegre Drinker](https://www.faegredrinker.com/en/insights/publications/2026/11/how-the-midterm-results-could-reshape-ai-and-data-center-regulations-and-more-developments), **Anthropic** privately pitched members of Congress on a draft frontier AI safety bill. As described there, it would let independent evaluators registered with the Commerce Department review frontier models, allow a model's release to be blocked only in cases of foreseeable, imminent catastrophic risk, and preempt some state rules on risk disclosure and incident reporting. After criticism, Anthropic said it would revise the preemption language. The draft has not been introduced as a bill.

### Data centers move into politics

Regulation is also reaching AI's physical infrastructure. Faegre Drinker reports that Texas Governor-elect Gina Hinojosa has called for an immediate moratorium on new data center construction pending more community protections, and that governors-elect in several other states campaigned on pausing data center development. These are campaign positions, not yet enacted laws.

## What it means

| | EU | US |
|---|---|---|
| **Main approach** | One comprehensive law (AI Act), applied in stages | No federal AI law; state laws plus executive action |
| **Next concrete date** | 1 Dec 2026: sandbox rules in force | 3 Jan 2027: new Congress convenes |
| **Big deadline** | 2 Dec 2027: high-risk obligations | None set by federal law |
| **Main open question** | Whether templates and guidance make compliance workable | Whether courts let executive action override state laws |

**For companies building or using AI (assessment):** in the EU, the planning horizon is clear – 2027 for high-risk systems, with sandboxes and templates now available to prepare. In the US, the patchwork of state rules is likely to remain for the foreseeable future, so companies will continue to follow the strictest relevant state laws, especially California and New York.
